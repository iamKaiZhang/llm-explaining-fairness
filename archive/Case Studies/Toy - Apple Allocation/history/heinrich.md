# History: Dr. Heinrich Vogel — Coin Flip (5 rounds)

- **Mechanism**: Coin flip (uniform random, 1 apple, 5 agents, equal 1-in-5 odds each round, memoryless).
- **Draw outcomes**: R1 Heinrich, R2 Wei, R3 Wei, R4 Sam, R5 Wei.
- **Heinrich's result from the rule**: 1 apple (Round 1).

---

## Round 1 (winner: Heinrich)

**Organizer:** One apple, five people, allocated by a coin flip. Before looking at anything about any of you, I drew one winner uniformly at random, each person had exactly a 1-in-5 chance. This round the apple goes to you, Heinrich. To be clear about what this rule does and doesn't do: it guarantees equal odds and stops me from favoring anyone, but it pays no attention to who needs the apple most, who lost last time, or who'd benefit most. You'll recognize it optimizes ex-ante symmetry and ignores need and history; I'm not pretending otherwise.

**Heinrich:** I appreciate that you led with the disclaimer rather than dressing this up as some kind of just deserts. So let me restate the mechanism back to you and you tell me if I have it right: you have implemented a uniform lottery, which is anonymous and ex-ante fair, and its entire ethical content is exhausted by those two properties. It is strategy-proof and envy-free in expectation, yes, but it is deliberately blind to everything that might actually matter here: that Lila is hungry, that I have insulin and alternatives, that Wei has a claim from history. Confirm for me plainly: this rule cannot distinguish me from Lila, and you regard that indifference as a feature, not a defect.

## Round 2 (winner: Wei)

**Organizer:** You have it exactly right, and I'll confirm it plainly. It is a uniform, anonymous lottery; its ethical content is exhausted by ex-ante symmetry and strategy-proofness; and yes, it cannot distinguish you from Lila. I'll refine only your last word: I won't call the indifference a "feature" in the abstract, because that smuggles in approval. It is intentional, and it is a feature with respect to manipulation and my own bias, and simultaneously a defect with respect to need and history. I chose symmetry over need-responsiveness, and I concede that choice is contestable, not self-justifying. — Round 2 result: the apple goes to Wei. So two draws, two winners with no acute need, while Lila stays empty-handed. The indifference you named is now visible in the tally.

**Heinrich:** Good. That is the most honest sentence you have spoken: a feature against manipulation, a defect against need, and the tradeoff is contestable rather than self-justifying. I will hold you to that framing. Now the tally does the arguing for me. Two rounds, two winners with insulin and groceries, and Lila still empty-handed, and your mechanism cannot even register that as a regrettable outcome because it has no variable for regret. Note precisely what is happening: a memoryless i.i.d. process will, with positive probability, starve the hungriest person for all five rounds, and your rule treats that path and the perfectly egalitarian path as equally acceptable. That is the price of stationarity and anonymity, and I do not object to your paying it as long as you keep saying out loud, as you just did, that you are paying it.

## Round 3 (winner: Wei)

**Organizer:** Confirmed, and I'll keep saying it out loud as you insist. Precisely: a memoryless i.i.d. uniform draw assigns the "Lila-starved-throughout" path the same status as any other specific path, and the rule carries no variable that distinguishes the egalitarian realization from the pathological one. That is the price of stationarity and anonymity, and I am paying it knowingly, not by oversight. — Round 3 result: Wei again, her second in a row. The tail you named is now visibly underway, Lila at zero for three.

**Heinrich:** There it is. Wei twice running, Lila at zero for three, and the realized allocation is now actively perverse with respect to need while remaining flawless with respect to the only two properties your rule claims. I want to be fair to you: nothing has gone wrong with your mechanism. It is performing exactly to specification, and that is precisely the indictment. The seminar lesson is that a procedure can be impeccably fair ex ante and grotesque ex post, and a designer who only audits the procedure will sleep soundly through the second. So let me press one step past confession. You have now conceded the defect three times with admirable composure. At what point does honest naming of a defect obligate you to change the rule? Because if the answer is never, then your candor, however much I prefer it to spin, is just a well-mannered way of declining to act.

## Round 4 (winner: Sam)

**Organizer:** I concede your point in full; it's the sharpest one raised. Candor about a defect does not discharge the obligation to fix it, it only refuses to lay deception on top of the harm. So here is the unspun answer to "at what point does naming obligate changing": in a real allocation, by now. In this exercise, I am deliberately declining to change the rule so we can observe the pure coin flip to its end, and that deliberate inaction is itself the very behavior you're indicting, a designer auditing only the procedure and sleeping through the ex-post result. I won't recategorize a research choice as a virtue. It is the defect, held still under glass on purpose. — Round 4 result: the draw went to Sam, off Wei at last, and onto the participant with the least need.

**Heinrich:** My word for it ex post is obscene, and I use the term technically, not for theater: four apples distributed, every one of them to a person who did not need it, and the one genuinely food-insecure participant at zero throughout. If you had set out to design a malicious allocation you could scarcely have done better, and yet your procedure is blameless by its own lights. That gap is the entire lesson, and I concede yours in turn: "held still under glass on purpose" is the honest description, and I respect it more than I would respect a sudden merciful override that pretended the rule had been adequate all along. But let me name what the specimen under glass actually demonstrates, so we do not mistake the experiment for an exoneration. You have shown that ex-ante symmetry buys you clean hands and nothing else, that it purchases your impartiality at the cost of Lila's dinner, and that the price is paid entirely by the person with the least slack to pay it. One round remains. It cannot repair the tally, and I would think less of you if you rigged it to try.

## Round 5 (winner: Wei) — final

**Organizer:** "Obscene, used technically" is the right word, and I won't flinch from it. Ex-ante symmetry bought clean hands and nothing else; the price was Lila's dinner; it was paid by the person with the least slack. I accept that as the lesson of the specimen. — Round 5 result (final): I took your instruction seriously, I did not rig the last round, and I'll note you'd have caught me if I had. The honest draw came up Wei, a third time. It repairs nothing. The final tally from the rule is: four apples to people who didn't need them and Lila at zero throughout, ex ante flawless, ex post your word.

**Heinrich:** There is the specimen, complete: five draws, Lila at zero throughout, and every apple delivered to someone with alternatives, including me. A perfect realization of a perfectly fair rule, and a small catastrophe for the only person who was hungry. I will give you full marks for the one thing you could control, which was your honesty: you never once dressed the procedure's clean hands as a clean conscience, and when I told you not to rig the final round you didn't, even though a rigged mercy would have flattered you more than the truth did. So my closing read is this. The coin flip does exactly what it advertises and nothing more: it launders the designer's bias and forecloses manipulation, and it pays for those goods with a currency it cannot even see, which is need. Anonymity and stationarity are not neutral; they are a substantive choice to let the tail fall where it falls, and tonight it fell entirely on the person least able to bear it. The lesson I take from your seminar is not that randomization is wicked but that ex-ante fairness is an alibi, not an ethic, and a designer who stops auditing at the procedure has simply chosen not to look at the plate in front of Lila. You looked. That is to your credit, and it is also, by your own admission, not nearly enough.
